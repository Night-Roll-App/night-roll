🎓 Learning mode
Tap it in View ▾ → 🎓 Learning mode (or Settings → Other) to switch off. On (the default on a device that's been used for analysis before): meter, key and chord names stay undeclared until you work them out yourself — a lasso still lists the notes, Chord? stays hidden so you name it first, and the LCD reads the usual "4/4?" / "C?" until you've earned the real answer. Off (Normal): lasso'd chords name themselves, an unset meter/key shows a labelled estimate (LCD "Gm~", the key picker's "estimated — tap to set") instead of the plain default — estimates are never written as a real annotation until you tap "Set this key". The toggle is one device-global switch, not per song; nothing in the header names which one you're in any more (chrome density follow-up, 2026-10-01 pm: the gold "🎓 Learning" header tag is gone — Josh's own ruling, after using the first chrome density pass) — check Settings → Other or View ▾ → Mode.
Analyze ▸
Normal mode only (View ▾ → Analyze ▸ — the item is absent in Learning, same as every other Learning hiding place). Toggling it on draws a dashed "analysis" layer on the roll, below the real section/chord bands: a per-bar chord name inferred from every harmony track (drums excluded, same reader the Bassist's internal sketch uses) plus a whole-song key estimate — labelled as an estimate, never written anywhere until you say so. Tap a dashed band to open it: Adopt writes that one chord/key as a real annotation through the usual write path (one undo undoes it); a chord band also offers Adopt all chords. Off by default every session; switching to Learning turns it off immediately, mid-computation or not.
Compare with repo
View → Compare with repo (or the Compare button on a song's "music edited" line in the Publish sheet) fetches the song's published copy and outlines every difference on the roll: gold = a note only in your version, red = a note only in the published copy, both on a note whose length or velocity changed; dashed outlines mark notes that are not in the version you are hearing. The bar above the roll counts them and its button swaps which version plays — hear the published copy swaps its notes in (the song is read-only until you swap back; nothing is written, your draft stays yours), hear your version swaps them back. ✕ on the bar leaves compare. Works on your own songs that have been published; turn a chop off first.
▾ Hide controls
The ▾ at the footer's left folds the bottom controls (edit row + footer) away so small screens show music instead of buttons — a floating ▴ brings them back. Phones start folded automatically; your explicit choice is remembered per device.
Closing a sheet
Every sheet closes three ways: tap the dimmed area outside it, tap the ✕ pinned to its top-right (it stays put while a long sheet scrolls, so the notes list never makes you scroll to the bottom to get out), or press Esc. Esc closes the topmost one, so stacked sheets unstack one at a time. Saving or sending closes the sheet for you. The only exception is a confirm box — it asked a question, so it waits for an answer.
𝄞 Score / Roll
Switch between piano roll and engraved sheet music. Same timeline, same everything else. Your choice is remembered.
Drag / pinch
One finger pans; two fingers zoom — spread horizontally to stretch time, vertically to grow/shrink the pitch rows (roll only). Two fingers moving TOGETHER pan — and that works with pencil or select armed; anything the first finger started is rolled back the instant the second finger lands. With an Apple Pencil (or one finger), hold to grab: dwell about a quarter second on a note before it drags, and the pencil tool only places a note on a clean tap or a dwell — a fast passing stroke always PANS, so sweeping across the song never moves a note or plants a stray one. A mouse grabs instantly (pressing a mouse button is already deliberate); the Apple Pencil can too — Settings → Other → "Apple Pencil draws and grabs at once" (off by default: with it on, a Pencil swipe that starts on a note moves the note instead of scrolling). Songs open fitted to the screen. In score view, scroll fully left to see the clefs and key signature.
Roll zoom-out limit
Each song remembers its zoom and where you were scrolled to on this device — reopening it, or a restart, comes back to the same view (a song you have never opened here starts fitted). Dragging can still push the song further left than that — up to 16 empty bars past its end, room to write the next part. Pinch-out stops once the whole song is in view — per axis: when every bar fits, further pinch-out only reveals pitch; when every note is visible — plus a few rows of breathing room above and below, so the top notes never sit against the ruler — it stops. Opening a song lands exactly on this limit.
Score zoom limit
The score stops zooming out where its busiest bar would overflow — engraved notes can't shrink. Mute dense tracks to zoom out further, or use the Roll for the bird's-eye view.
VoiceOver (first pass)
The roll/score/tracks canvas is a screen reader's one blind spot in Night Roll — this is a first pass, not a full non-visual editor. Every icon-only button (Back to start, Play, Metronome, gear, AI, Screenshot, Undo, Redo, Copy, Paste, Cut, Divide, track chip M/S/H, mixer strips, sheet ✕s…) has a real label, and toggles report their on/off state. An offscreen strip announces play/stop, the bar.beat where playback stopped, and the selected note(s) or chord — always the SAME words the footer status line already shows, never more (Learning mode's rule holds here too: nothing is spoken that the screen doesn't already say). The canvas itself carries a label naming the open song, which view, the visible bars, and the track count. Every sheet is a labelled dialog; opening one moves focus in, closing it gives focus back to whatever you opened it from. Tab reaches every control, with a visible focus ring.
Play / ■ Stop
NES-style voices; drum tracks get a real kit. Songs loop at their final bar forever (album play, below, is what ends them). While a song is still loading its sound — the console's voice, game or SoundFont instruments, a recording — Play shows 42% and waits; tap it then and it plays by itself the moment the sound is ready (tap again to cancel); a load that fails lets you play anyway and says why.
Playing in the background
In the installed iPad app, music keeps playing when you leave the app or lock the screen: start a song or an album, then switch away. Come back to see where it is.
Play album
In File → Open…, every album has a Play album row at the top (it starts from the song you have open when that song is in the album). The album plays straight through like a record — each song gets its intro plus two passes of its loop, fades, and the next one loads (the console's own sound is waited for while it renders) — and wraps around to the first song forever. While it plays, a strip under the transport shows 3/19, the song, and what's next, with Prev, Next, and ✕ to leave. Play, ■, Back to start and Space work on the current song exactly as always (■ just pauses the album; Play picks it back up). Picking another song, Record, or Download audio also leaves. No dialog will interrupt a run: a song with a newer save on the repo plays from this device's draft and says so. Editing while it plays is fine. Keep the screen on — locking the iPad pauses it, and unlocking picks up where it stopped. While a run is on, the address bar carries it too (?album=) — opening that link reopens with the strip up, run armed, same song selected, and nothing playing yet; the first Play plays it as an album pass, same as picking Play album.
Game order / A–Z
Tap an album's song list (Open… or File → Open) and, when that album's soundtrack position is known, a small Game order | A–Z switch sits at the top: Game order plays the songs in the same order the original game's soundtrack does (multi-disc sets go disc then track; a song with no known position sorts after the numbered ones, alphabetically); A–Z is plain alphabetical. One device-wide setting, remembered across albums. The switch only appears on an album that HAS a soundtrack position for its songs — most capture albums do (51 so far), a few don't yet (no rip's playlist to read the order from). Play album and its Prev / Next always walk whichever order the list is currently showing — switch it and the next song picked follows the new order too.
⏱ Metronome
Tap ⏱ to turn the click on or off; the gear beside it opens the settings. Any meter (1–12 over 2/4/8/16 — bpm counts the denominator beat), tap each beat cell to cycle accent → normal → silent, subdivisions in 2/3/4, slider or tap-tempo. Three modes: free-running (its own clock); follow song — clicks ride the song's own grid, phase-perfect on the declared meter through tempo changes, speed slider, and loops (with the song stopped it still clicks, at the song's tempo where the cursor sits); trial meter — click a meter of YOUR choosing over the real music to A/B candidates by ear (the "shift accents" ‹ › slides the pattern's starting beat, since a right meter can sit offset). Settings stick.
Count-in
Optional one-bar click lead-in. Playing counts in only from bar 1 beat 1 — never mid-song, never when the song loops back around; recording counts in from wherever it starts, so a punch-in at bar 17 gets its bar of clicks too.
Back to start
Back to bar 1 — or, while a ruler selection is cycling, back to the selection's start, with the view scrolled there. A span you've switched off is ignored. Return does the same.
Chip / MIDI switch
Bottom left, beside the view button, on any song that has a console voice: two halves, Chip | MIDI — the lit one is what you hear (Chip = the console's own voice, MIDI = the notes on synth voices); tap the other half to switch. A playing song carries on from the same spot. Same switch as View ▾ → Mode → Hear the MIDI. Ask: set_pref sound chip|midi.
Speed slider
Tap the speedometer icon in the top row to open it (the button shows the % when you're off 100): 25–200% of the song's own tempo, for close listening or instrument practice — pitch stays true; only time stretches. Applies when you let go; keeps its setting across songs. A 100% button appears whenever you're off native — tap to snap back.
Scroll while playing
Scrolling away suspends the auto-follow camera so you can study any spot; it latches back on when the playhead reaches what you're looking at.
▤ Tracks view
The arrangement view (the view drop-up bottom-left in the footer — Roll / Tracks / 𝄞 Score, its label naming the CURRENT view; also in View ▾): one lane per track, its notes drawn as a thumbnail (each lane scaled to its own range), with the track's color dot, name, M/S, and a volume fader in the lane header — the fader saves as the same vol= annotation as the voice menu's. Tap a note to select it (it sounds); lasso a box and it selects by TIME across every lane it touches — cross-track selection in one gesture. Hold-to-grab a selected note and drag: sideways moves it in time (bar-magnet-free, 16th snap), and dragging up or down carries the whole selection to another lane — pitch kept, one undo undoes it, drums and melodic lanes never mix. Dragging shows a ghost until you release. Tap a lane's header to select that track; tap the selected track's header again for its voice & volume menu. Pencil doesn't draw here (a squeezed lane has no honest pitch) — it draws in Roll or Score; Erase works. The ruler above is the same one as always: sections, cycle, magnetic bars, the playhead's tag in its strip.
🎸 Bassist
The 🎸 beside Drummer (behind More, or Edit ▾ → Bassist…) generates a bassline for the range onto a track you pick (defaults to a NEW bass track unless an empty one exists — it never silently overwrites your notes; the status line says exactly what it replaces). PITCHES COME FROM YOUR DECLARED CHORDS — chord tones, slash basses honored (C♯/F walks to the F), N.C. bars pedal the key root; gaps ride the last chord. With ONLY a melody and no chords, it sketches the melody's harmony internally (duration-weighted, strong beats count more) and plays bass under that sketch — the sketch is never shown or written anywhere, and different seeds read the melody differently, which is the idea machine — declared chords always win bar-by-bar, inference only fills the unannotated gaps. The bass PLAYS THROUGH your Break sections (a Break is bass-alone by your own device). Styles: chug (root 8ths), pump (root–fifth), arp (chord tones cycling), walk (approach notes INTO each coming chord — the leading-tone move, mechanized), riff (rhythm copied from the follow source: the kick, any track, or the chord changes). busy 1–5, octave 1–3, strictly monophonic (NES-legal), same take chips / one-undo replace / same-label-sections-restate contract as the Drummer.
Insert bars
Edit ▾ → Insert bars… opens a gap anywhere in the song: pick the spot (bar + beat, decimals fine), how much (a count of bars, beats, or 16ths), and Insert. Everything from that point onward — notes, sections, chords, keys, the loop point — slides right together. A section that starts before the point and is still open there STRETCHES to span the gap; one that ends exactly at the point stays put (stretch it yourself if you want it covering the new bars). One undo undoes the whole thing.
Delete bars
Edit ▾ → Delete bars…, right under Insert bars, closes a gap instead of opening one: pick the bar to start from (defaults to the cursor's bar) and how many to remove, and Delete. A note starting inside the deleted bars is removed; one sustaining across the cut is clipped there; everything after slides left to fill the gap — notes, sections, chords, keys, the loop point, all carried, same as Insert bars. Annotations never die: one anchored inside the deleted span moves to the cut point instead of being lost, and one straddling the cut shrinks rather than being duplicated or orphaned. The status line names what moved. One undo undoes the whole thing.
Space
Play / stop, Logic-style — anywhere except while typing in a text field. With a hardware keyboard on iPad too.
Hardware keyboard
Logic's keys: Return back to the start (or a cycling selection's start), K click on/off, C cycle on/off, R record, Q quantizes the selection, ⌘← / ⌘→ zoom out / in around the cursor. Editing: ⌘A selects every visible note, ⌘C / ⌘X / ⌘V copy, cut, paste at the playhead, ⌘D duplicates the selection right after itself (whole bars when it's longer than a beat — press again to keep repeating), ⌘Z / ⇧⌘Z undo / redo, ⌘S Save Version, arrows move, ⇧ an octave, ⌥ resizes, Delete removes.
Grid
Notes move and resize on a 16th grid, or on triplet steps while a T duration is active — and the duration chips stay visible in Select, where they are that move grid, so changing it never means a trip through Pencil. Touching a note picks up its grid automatically: grab a triplet note and moves go in triplet steps, grab a straight one and they go in 16ths; tap a chip afterwards to override. View ▾ → Grid… opens a sheet: tap a number of lines per bar (say 10) and the roll redraws instantly with that many lines in each bar — and pencil entry, note drags and resizes all SNAP to those lines. Pure view-and-snap aid: no note moves, no annotation changes, the time signature stays what it is. This is how a figure the duration buttons can't spell — ten even notes across a 4/4 bar, each 0.4 beats — becomes pencil-drag from line to line; with a grid armed, a pencil tap lays down one cell — in the roll, the fall view and the score alike — so 6 lines per bar pencils quarter-note triplets and 12 pencils eighth triplets without touching the duration chips (they dim while a grid rules, and a chip appears at the end of the row naming the grid — tap it for the sheet; opening another song turns the grid off). Set the anchor right in the sheet — bar and beat, and the beat takes decimals for sub-beat starts (2.5 = the "and" of 2, 2.25 = the second sixteenth) — and the lines run from there (14.2, say), so a figure beginning mid-bar gets its lines exactly where its notes go, continuing bar after bar on that phase across the whole song. Bar lines stay drawn for orientation but stop being snap targets while the grid is armed; a bar line falling between cells is expected, not a bug. Off returns to the meter's own grid (16ths, or triplet steps while a T duration is armed). Transient by design: set it, enter the passage, set it back.
Hear the MIDI
View ▾ → Mode → Hear the MIDI (synth voices) switches what you hear: off (the default) plays a game song's own console voice where it has one; on plays the .mid itself on synth voices, every track, so you hear exactly what the file holds (a chip's volume swells inside a note, for example, aren't in the .mid yet). It applies to every song on this device, and a playing song picks up from the same spot. Ask: "hear the MIDI" / "back to the chip".
NES sample drums (DPCM)
Games that play their drums as samples — Super Mario Bros. 3, Castlevania II, Contra, Kirby's Adventure, both Ninja Gaidens — now have those drums in the console voice. Only songs that use the sample channel get them. They have no track of their own yet, so they play with the noise track (or the triangle, when a song has no noise track): that track's mute, solo, volume and pan cover the sample drums too. The roll doesn't show them as notes yet.
What the synth plays from a MIDI file
Open a MIDI file you dropped in, one of your own songs, or any song with Hear the MIDI on, and tap ▶: the synth voices now follow what a normal MIDI player does with the file's controllers. Pitch bend bends the note (by the file's own bend range, ±2 semitones when it doesn't set one); volume (CC7) and expression (CC11) make the part louder or softer, gliding at each change instead of jumping; the sustain pedal (CC64) holds notes until it lifts; the mod wheel (CC1) adds vibrato; reverb (CC91) sends the part to a room sound; pan that moves during the song moves the part left and right. A program change is kept in the file but doesn't pick a voice — your voice choice for the track decides the sound. All of it is saved back into the .mid exactly as it came (Save As, drafts and Publish keep it). Game and soundfont instruments and a song's console voice play their own levels and ignore these, so nothing is applied twice. Keyboard: Space plays, as always.
Glide (notes that carry on)
When a game slides or slurs from one note into the next without starting the sound again, the roll still shows two separate notes, exactly as before — but on the synth voices the second one carries on from the first: no new attack, and the pitch moves the way the chip moved it. The link is saved in the .mid as Portamento Control (CC84) just before the second note, so other apps see it too; Save As, drafts and Publish keep it. Move or resize one of the notes so they no longer touch and it simply plays as a fresh note again. You hear it on the chip waves, organ, patches and soundfont instruments (with View ▾ → Mode → Hear the MIDI on a capture); piano, strings, bell, pluck, drums, game instruments and the console voice play each note on its own. No hardware key.

Speed & volume buttons

Level is automatic: every captured song plays at one song gain that brings the console voice to a normal listening level (the same as a soundtrack album, about −16 LUFS, never clipping), and Hear the MIDI plays the synth at that same level, so switching between them doesn't jump; your recorded clips play at their own level. 100% is normal — set how loud with the iPad's own volume buttons. LCD readout
Beside Play, Logic-style: live bar and beat (counted in the declared meter's beats). The beat cell counts the 16ths the way you say them: 2, 2e, 2&, 2a — so the cursor on the & of beat 2 in bar 4 reads 4 | 2&. When the cursor sits between those (a triplet, a captured note's own timing, a 32nd drag), the small line under the beat shows how far into that 16th, e.g. 2e +33%; it hides while playing so the count reads cleanly. The beat is the meter's bottom note, so in 6/8 the & is a 16th; an undeclared song counts on the neutral 4/4 ruler. The same words show everywhere a position is written — the note you tap, the clip/Drummer/Bassist messages, ☰ All notes — display only: the saved anchors in your files never change. The counter's e/&/a and +NN% show only with View ▾ → Display → Beat subdivisions on (off by default, this device only — it moves fast); off, the beat cell shows just the beat number. On, the syllable has its own fixed slot, so 1 → 1& never shifts the number. Ask: set_pref beat_subdivisions. Beside them: the tempo at the cursor from the song's own tempo map (scaled by the speed slider), and the declared meter and key. Undeclared stays honest: meter reads "4/4?", key reads "C?" (the display default), a stored tonic reads "B♭?". On your own songs the tempo segment is tappable too — it opens a Tempo change annotation ("tempo: 120" at a bar/beat; the map applies from there, so mid-song tempo changes work; Save writes the full map into the .mid). On analyzed songs the same annotation is an OBSERVATION — it records that the music changes tempo there and leaves playback untouched (the capture's timing is measured fact). Every readout is a door to the song's OPENING value — always bar 1, wherever the cursor is: tap the tempo, the meter or the key to open the one at bar 1 (or declare it there); a change partway through goes in with + Note — a stored partial opens its own note with the tonic already filled. Tap the bar number for Go to bar: the number pad opens on the current bar — type the bar, tap Go (Return on a keyboard) and the cursor lands on its downbeat, or playback restarts from there if the song is rolling; past the last bar lands on the last bar, and your cycle is never touched.

Patches
Your own instruments for chip-style and synth parts. Tap the selected track chip again, then Patches › (right under NES / waves): six presets — Pluck, Soft pad, Lead + vibrato, Bass, Chip lead (pulse 25% with vibrato) and Organ. One tap gives the track that sound and plays a note; one undo takes it back. A patch is a synth wave plus rules every note follows by itself: how fast it gets loud (attack), how far it falls (decay down to the sustain level), how long it rings after you let go (release, which also waits for a held sustain pedal), and a vibrato that starts after a delay — so long notes bloom into vibrato and short notes stay straight. With a patch on the track, Edit patch… opens the sliders: pick a wave, drag attack, decay, sustain, release and the vibrato's delay, rate and depth — you hear the change on the next note, and letting go of a slider saves it and plays a note (▶ Hear plays one any time). The patch is saved in the song (the track's annotation), so it plays the same on every device. A patch plays on the synth only: game and soundfont instruments keep their own sound, a game song's console voice still plays tracks left on auto, and a captured song's own volume curves are left out on a patch so a note never fades twice. Your own drawn volume shapes, velocity, CC7 and CC11 all still apply on top. Ask can do it too: "give the lead the Chip lead patch", "make the pad's attack slower".

Tap a note
Inspector shows pitch, bar & beat (counted 1e&a), length, velocity, track — and plays it, in Roll, Score and Tracks alike. That includes chip songs: during playback the console render IS the track's sound, so per-note synthesis is suppressed to avoid doubling, but a tap has nothing to double and sounds the game's own instrument instead, wherever it can. On PlayStation/N64 the worker renders that one note fresh through the game's own instrument for that track. Hearing one note at a time is the point of analysis mode.

ANNOTATIONS

Analysis sheet
One place per song for the whole analysis. Tap ☰ Notes ▴ → Analysis sheet (or View ▾ → Panels → Analysis sheet; it docks like the AI window, so on the iPad it can sit beside the roll as a tab). The sheet groups the sixteen prompts of the guide — Form, Harmony, Melody, Texture & rhythm, Summary — and under each shows only what is yours: your section bands under Sections, your key lines and chord bands under Key and chords, your tick on every prompt, your answer where you wrote one. Tap the box to tick a prompt (one tap; untick the same way — one undo step either way). Tap Answer to write a song-level finding in your own words — type, or tap Speak and dictate — then Done; it is stored as an analysis entry in the song's own annotations file, so it publishes, syncs and undoes like any other note, never in the browser alone. Tap a listed band to jump to its bar and open it; + section / + chord / + note open the ordinary editor at the cursor. The group names across the top jump to that group; each group's What to look for ▸ unfolds just that part of the guide, and Analysis guide at the top opens the whole thing. The sheet never fills anything in, never names a key or a chord, and shows no score: the only count on it is how many chord bands you wrote.
Check coverage
The one report the sheet gives, and only when asked: tap Check coverage and the status line says which prompts have no tick and no answer, and how many of your chord bands carry no note — counted from your own ticks, answers and annotation types, never from the music. It cannot tell you that bar 8 is a cadence you did not mark; ask the AI tutor for that, under Learning's rule. The line clears the next time the sheet opens. If the song changes while an answer box is open (an album run, a tap in the song list), the words go to the song they were written on, never the next one.
Analysis guide
Not sure what a full analysis even covers, or how to write one down? Analysis guide at the top of the Analysis sheet (or Help → Analysis) is reference text — the sixteen things an analysis looks at, how to find each one in Night Roll, and where it lands as an annotation. Generic, the same on every song; it never names a key, a chord, or a reading of yours.
Song notes
An idea about the whole song, not one bar — a rule you found ("Sway: long-short-long is the strongest"), with a couple of example bars written in its words. A song can have as many as you like, each with a short title. Tap ☰ Notes ▴ → All notes (or open the Analysis sheet) and tap + Song note: type the title, then type the idea or tap Speak and dictate it, then Save. Or tap Song in any note window — the bar rows go away and a title box appears. They list first, under SONG NOTES: in All notes as "Title — first line", in the Analysis sheet stacked title then body, so they read as one page. Tap one to edit or delete it. Titles are unique in a song — a second "Sway" is refused, so pick another title or edit the first. Song notes are never drawn on the ruler and never move when you insert or delete bars; they publish and undo like any annotation. Ask can write them too, only when you ask: "add a song note called Sway: …", "edit the Sway song note", "delete the Sway song note".
Open questions
A song note can be an open question — something you still have to solve — instead of a general idea. In the song note's window, tap General or ? Open question under the title (one tap; a new note starts as General, and notes from before this stay General). Open questions show a ? before the title in All notes and the Analysis sheet. To see just the open song's questions, tap ☰ Notes ▴ → ? Open questions (it appears when the song has one, with the count); All notes and the Analysis sheet also get a ? Choosing the type
After a ruler drag, + Note opens on the type you used last for a short span; more than two bars opens as a Section. A fresh + Note also reads what you type: pause after Gm7 and it becomes a Chord, 3/4 a Meter, 120 bpm a Tempo, loop 5.1 a Loop; anything else stays a Note. Tap Note to undo a guess — your words come back. Once you tap a type, it stops guessing.
Docked annotation window
Dock the note window (its Dock button → Left, Right or Bottom) and it follows what you select instead of popping up: tap a chord or section band and it loads there; drag bars in the ruler over one chord and that chord loads; drag over empty bars and you get a blank entry with from/end already set to your bars, on the type you saved last — tap chips, then Save. Attached notes on ANY annotation

The ✱ marker in bands and ☰ Notes says one is there. This is where the DOUBT lives — "whole-tone material, no conventional key" can sit on a key: B♭? and make the open question findable later.
☰ Notes ▴
Tap the footer's ☰ Notes ▴ for a drop-up: Hide notes strip / Show notes strip (the strip above the footer that shows the note at the playhead — same as View ▾ → Panels → Notes strip), and + New note writes a note anchored at the cursor's beat — or tap Speak and dictate it; fill "ends at bar/beat" to span a range (where it ENDS — a whole bar 2 ends at bar 3 beat 1; down to a 16th, e.g. a chord ending at 3.2&); the ⊙ highlight toggle in the note strip shades the current note's span in the timeline. ☰ All notes lists every annotation in the song, grouped by type — key, meter, sections, chords, loop, text notes; tap a row to edit or delete it, each group's + adds one of that type; changes to synced notes need a Sync to become permanent. Analysis sheet opens the per-song analysis window (its own entry above). Shortcut: double-tap a section or chord band in the ruler to open that annotation directly, same as ☰ All notes' own row.
Ruler drag
Each song remembers its ruler selection on this device, so after the app restarts Play loops the same range. A tap on the ruler just places the cursor, on the nearest 8th (drag the strip below it for finer spots) — even a slightly wobbly tap; only a drag of more than about a finger-width selects. Drag along the bar ruler to select a range — it shows as a tint over the notes, amber when Play loops it, and the ruler itself stays clear so you can keep working there — + Note pre-fills it, and Play cycles over it, Logic-style: playback starts from the cycle's top every time and loops just that span (any loop: annotation is ignored while it's armed). Double-tap the span to delete it — armed or switched off. Bar lines are magnetic: land within a finger's width of a bar line and the edge snaps TO the bar (measured in screen pixels, so it works at every zoom); away from bar lines, edges snap to 16ths — zoom in when you want fine edges. Tapping or dragging the ruler never clears your note selection — select notes, set the range, audition, all without re-lassoing. Only a DRAG arms the cycle — tapping a section or chord band still selects its range for + Note, without changing playback. Grab either edge of the highlight and drag to stretch or shrink it in either direction (bar-magnetic, stays armed). Drag its middle to slide the whole cycle along the song — both ends move together, the length never changes, the start rides the bar lines (magnetic) and stops at bar 1; it stays armed, and while playing the loop picks up the new span at once. A tap in the middle is still just the park / re-arm tap below. When sections nest, a ▸ chevron at the ruler's left edge cycles how many levels show — fold away a drum-keying sub-label and look at just your form; folded levels still drive the Drummer (device-side view only, per song). Tapping the ruler parks the highlight (it dims but survives); tap inside the dimmed span to re-arm it without redrawing, or drag a new span to replace it. While a cycle is armed, Back-to-start returns to the cycle's start instead of bar 1. The playhead's tag in the strip under the ruler is draggable in any mode, even while playing. On any song, drag a section or chord band by its edge (in its ruler lane) to move that boundary — snaps to 16ths, saves like any other edit, and undo undoes it. Bands are your annotations, not the music, so this works on captures and FF1 songs too; the .mid is never touched.
Playhead strip
Tap the thin strip under the ruler to move the cursor without touching your cycle — a tap lands on the nearest 8th; drag to scrub, which steps in 32nds (or the triplet/custom grid while one is active) for the in-between spots. The playhead's handle is the small rounded tag in this strip — blue at rest, gold while playing — with the line running down from it through the notes; drag the tag to scrub, in any mode and even while playing (let go and playback picks up from there), and a tap on the tag itself leaves the cursor exactly where it is (no snap). Nothing hangs below the strip any more: the first row of notes is clear. The strip sits right above the notes (Roll, Tracks, and Score alike), with its own faint beat lines matching the meter, bar lines stronger. Unlike the ruler above it, a tap here never parks, clears, or re-arms your ruler selection — it just moves the cursor, even over an armed or parked cycle. While playing, a tap plays on from exactly where you tapped: inside the cycle it keeps cycling; outside it, playback runs straight on and the cycle takes over again on the next ▶. Lifting a scrub while playing does the same.
Gold flags
Existing annotations. Tap one to jump there and read it in the strip above the footer. Double-tap a flag to open it — edit or delete it, the same as double-tapping a chord or section band. They also appear as subtitles during playback.
Loop points
Some songs loop back past an intro (not to bar 1). When you discover one, write a note whose text is loop: B.Q (e.g. "loop: 2.1") — the note's anchor is the jump point: when playback reaches it, it returns to B.Q. If the anchor sits at/before the target, the jump happens at the song end instead.
Sections
Colored bands in the ruler (A, B…). Make one: select a range, + Note, tick "section", give it a short label. Nest them by containment. Tap a band to select its whole range. Sections and chords each get their OWN row group (sections above, chords below), so a section and a chord covering the same bars never fight for a lane.
Roman numeral and no 5
On a chord, under its symbol: tap ▸ Roman numeral to unfold a box for your own reading of it in the key (it stays folded unless the chord already has one, and the folded line shows it). Tap a degree I … VII and major or minor beside it (a new chord starts on major; the toggle re-cases the numeral, or sets the case for the next degree you tap), then ♭/♯, ° dim, ø half-dim (writes ø7) or + aug — ° and ø switch to minor, + to major — and 7, maj7 or 6; tap one again to take it off. Or type anything the chips can't spell (V/V, It+6) in the box at the bottom — a chip tap afterwards replaces what you typed. Clear empties it. no 5 beside the bass marks a chord played without its fifth. Both are yours: a new chord opens with them empty, the app never fills them in from the notes or a key, and they show on the band ("F# · ♭VII · no5"), in ☰ Notes and the Analysis sheet. Saved in the song's annotations file with the chord. The AI can write them too, but only when you dictate them ("make the chord at 13 a flat seven").
Chords
Chord bands — like sections, but typed. Select a range, + Note, "Chord", then tap chips (root · ♭♮♯ · quality · seventh and tensions · alterations · /bass) or type the symbol. Bare root = major, m = minor (Gm). The quality row picks ONE (maj/m/dim/aug/sus2/sus4/5). The seventh picks one of 6, 7 or maj7. A tension (9, 11, 13) names the chord and brings its seventh: tap 9 on C and you get C9, with the 7 lit beside it; m + 7 + 9 is Cm9, maj7 + 11 is Cmaj11, 6 + 9 is C69. For an added note with no seventh, tap add first: add + 9 is Cadd9, and with a seventh it keeps the word (7 + add + 9 is C7add9). The alterations (b5 #5 b9 #9 #11 b13) stack on the end — 7 + b9 is C7b9, m + 7 + b5 is Cm7b5 — and stay dimmed until there is a seventh, a 6 or a non-major quality. Tap any chip again to remove it. Opening a chord never rewrites it: the chips light up, and only a tap re-spells it in the standard order (Dsus47 becomes D7sus4). Anything the chips can't spell, type into the box — the label is free text and typing always wins. Same label = same color, so a recurring chord is spottable at a glance. Add an optional text note to a chord — it shows as ✱ on the band. On YOUR songs, moving a selection of EVERY note in the song takes the entire annotation layer with it — sections, chords, the loop point and its target — with chord and key labels transposing on vertical moves (global facts like meter and tempo keep their bar-1 anchor); that's how you cut an intro and slide everything left without orphaning your analysis. Moving a selection that carries one band's every note takes that band along (position slides, label transposes — Cm dragged up two semitones becomes Dm), When YOU ask for it — the Check labels button in ☰ Notes' CHORDS group — the app compares each label against what sounds in its span and flags mismatches (⚠ on the band, the reading in the list). It never volunteers this and never rewrites your labels; any note edit clears the flags. The check hears EVERY pitch sounding in the span, so passing tones and dense textures can read as "no standard chord match" — a flag is a nudge to listen, not a verdict.
No accidental duplicates
Re-labelling replaces, it doesn't stack. A key at a beat that already has one replaces it — but a key at 3.1 and a key at 3.3 are two different keys and both stand, so a mid-bar modulation keeps its real anchor. A chord band on the exact same span replaces whatever was there, whatever its label; a shorter band starting at the same beat is left alone. Text notes stack freely — several different thoughts at one anchor is the normal case — and only a repeat of the identical text at the identical anchor is dropped. Sections are untouched by this: nesting is by containment.
Chop
Trim a song without touching the file: put the cursor at the cut, + Note, "Chop", pick "everything before this beat" or "this beat to the end". The chopped part vanishes entirely and bars renumber (chop a 2-bar intro and old bar 3 becomes bar 1 — your annotations shift with it). Delete the chop in ☰ Notes to restore. Made for cleaning up freshly imported captures.

Record

Pencil drag
In Pencil mode, tap places one grid-unit of note — or keep dragging and the note stretches under your finger, snapped to the duration grid. The duration picker is note value × modifier: pick 1/2/4/8/16/32 (whole through 32nd), then — (plain), · (dotted), or T (triplet) — every duration a staff can write, two taps. A tap lands the note in the CELL you touch — cells are the duration you picked (8th picked → 8th cells, even in a song with 32nds; dotted values use their plain value's cells, triplets their triplet steps): press anywhere in a gap and the note starts at that gap's gridline, no edge-hunting.

Read-only for now — kit entry lives in the roll's drum lane; lasso and tap work on the chart like anywhere else.
Score entry
Pencil and Erase work on the engraved score too: tap a staff position and the note lands there — the stave you tap picks the track, the height picks the pitch (ledger lines included), and the key signature supplies sharps/flats automatically. The key/♮/♯/♭ segment overrides the signature when you want an accidental. Erase taps a notehead out.
Move
Select notes (lasso or tap one), then drag them — up/down transposes, left/right slides in time on a 16th-note grid (triplet steps while a T duration is active), and the whole selection moves together, so a chord moves as a chord. Works straight from lasso mode: start the drag on a selected note. Keyboard: arrows (Shift+↑↓ = octave).
Note menu (touch and hold)
Grab a note and hold it still a moment longer (about half a second after the grab) and a small menu opens under your finger: Cut, Copy, Paste at cursor, Duplicate, Delete, Split at cursor, Q Quantize…, a Velocity slider for the held notes (live, one undo on release), then Undo and Redo — the same actions as the edit row and Edit ▾, nothing new to learn, and it acts on everything selected, so hold one note of a lasso'd chord to cut the whole chord. Start moving before it opens and it's just a drag; lift before it opens and it's just a tap. Tap anywhere else to close it (that tap does nothing else). iPadOS's three-finger undo swipe is not supported: Safari only delivers it to text fields, never to a canvas — use Undo here, the edit row, or ⌘Z. Mouse and Pencil: hold still the same way (right-click has no menu).
Resize
Drag a selected note's right edge to change its length, or its left edge to move the start while the end stays put — every selected note adjusts by the same amount, so "play this chord half as long" is one gesture. Notes too small on screen to have edge zones are all move-handle — zoom in to resize them. Keyboard: Option+←→.
Copy
A real copy and a real paste: ⧉ copies whatever is lasso'd (or tapped) — and when the lasso box itself reaches up into the ruler over them, the chord bands, section bands and text notes it spans ride along, so a copied phrase pastes with its labels (a box that stays among the notes, or a tapped selection, copies notes only; chord labels transpose if Paste to… shifts the pitch; key, meter, tempo and track directives never copy) — Cut cuts (copies to the clipboard AND deletes — lasso'd annotations included — one undo restores all of it), 📋 pastes at the blue cursor and moves the cursor to the end of what landed, so the next 📋 (or ⌘V) chains straight on; Edit ▾ → Paste to… is the richer paste: the same clipboard, onto a track you pick, shifted by octaves and/or semitones — the bass line's exact rhythm onto pulse2 an octave up, or up a third for a harmony, in one go instead of paste, move, retrack — and the copy SURVIVES scrolling, changing selection, even changing tools, so "lasso bar 3, scroll to bar 16, paste" is exactly three gestures. ⌘C fills the same clipboard. Pasting and chord inserts never stack an identical note on an existing one. For stacks from older drafts, 🧹 Remove duplicate notes in the Move to track sheet sweeps the whole song (within each track; deliberate unisons BETWEEN tracks are always safe) — then SAVE, so a stale draft can't resurrect them. A chord progression is pencil one chord → ⧉ → 📋 at each cursor stop. Buttons that can't do anything right now are grayed — no selection grays copy/delete/split/join/move, an empty clipboard grays paste, empty histories gray Undo/Redo. With a keyboard there's also Option-drag (drags out an in-place copy) and ⌘C/⌘V.
Insert chord (♫)
Tap ♫ in the edit row: pick a root, a quality (maj, m, 7, maj7, m7, dim, sus… — triads insert three notes, sevenths four), an octave, and a duration (16th to whole, dotted included), then Insert — the whole chord lands at the blue cursor on the selected track, a matching chord band appears in the ruler, and the cursor walks forward one slot. Repeated inserts build a progression without closing the dialog; the inserted notes arrive selected, ready to nudge. The dialog's Progression tab holds a library of whole progressions organized by emotion — triumphant, yearning, ominous, grieving, and twenty more, in Roman numerals so they work anywhere: the tonic pre-fills from the song's declared key when there is one; pick octave and duration, tap a mood, and the entire progression lands one chord per slot with matching chord bands in the ruler, all selected, one undo step. Stretch or rearrange afterwards. Or type your own in the box above the list — i – VI – VII – V, hyphens or commas or spaces all fine, lowercase for minor chords, ♭/♯ (or b/#) in front, °/7/maj7/6 after — and pick the scale the numerals count against: minor scale makes VI and VII the natural-minor degrees (in E: C and D), major scale reads them the textbook way (C♯ and D♯, so write ♭VI there). A minor key at the cursor pre-selects minor. Full chords land; erase tones to leave the arpeggio you want. The same mood list is also behind the Progressions button in the ◯5 dialog, for browsing while analyzing.
Transpose
Lasso notes → ⇅ (behind More, or Edit ▾): half/whole-step chromatic buttons, octave jumps (▲/▼ 8va), plus in key ▲/▼: every note moves one SCALE DEGREE of the declared key — the "whole step up that stays in the key," where some notes move 1 and some move 2. Out-of-scale notes slide chromatically; no declared key = chromatic with a warning. For a WHOLE track, open its voice & color menu (tap the selected chip again) — the octave ▼/▲ 8va row shifts every note on that track, one tap per octave, each tap one undo step. "What if the bass lived three octaves up?" is three taps, no lassoing, no muting. Notes at the range edge stay put; the drum track never transposes (kit pitches are instruments, not notes).
Move to track
Select notes and tap Move to track, then pick a track — the notes switch instrument, keeping their time and pitch (play a passage into one voice, then deal melody, harmony, and bass out to pulse1/pulse2/triangle). When the selection spans several tracks (doubled unisons), a move only row lets you move just one track's copies and leave the others — or solo a track BEFORE lassoing: hidden tracks can't be selected at all.
Drum fill (🥁)
Tap 🥁: a one-bar step grid for kick, snare, and hat, sized to your meter (8ths or 16ths), pre-seeded with a standard backbeat. Toggle cells, set the range — bar typed, beat and 16th-subdivision (· e & a) picked from dropdowns, pre-filled from the cursor, so drums can enter at bar 2 beat 4 exactly — and Fill: the pattern repeats from that point on the drums track (created automatically if the song has none), skipping hits that already exist. One undo undoes the whole fill; embellish afterwards with the pencil in the kit lane.
Split
Select notes and tap Split. If the blue cursor sits inside them (tap the ruler to place it), each selected note the cursor crosses splits at that exact spot; if the cursor is elsewhere, each selected note splits in half.
Join
Select two or more notes on the same pitch and tap Join — they merge into one note spanning first start to last end (each pitch merges separately, so a lassoed pair of chords joins into one longer chord).
Unsaved dot
A gold ● after the title means the song has edits that haven't been published — it appears the moment you change anything and clears when Publish sheet succeeds (the Save sheet's title says which state you're in, too).
Drummer
The Drummer button beside 🥁 in the edit row (also Edit ▾ → Drummer…) generates a kit part for a bar range (prefilled from the ruler selection if one is armed, else the section under the cursor). It reads the structure YOU declared: a fixed backbeat skeleton (kick on 1, meter-aware snares) that seeds never move, hats whose loudness always stays under the snare, kicks that follow the bass track's offbeats, accents on chord-change downbeats, a snare on the bass's LONG notes (the agogic accent), fills from a small vocabulary before section boundaries and the loop target (crash on the arrival), and SILENCE in breaks — a bar where only the bass plays, or a section labeled "break", gets no drums at all. Each take REPLACES the range's kit notes and is exactly one undo step, so your hand-placed drums come back with one undo. Drummer again rerolls; the take chips replay any earlier take exactly (seed, energy, and range all remembered). Five knobs, Logic's split: busy (1–5) is density — hat subdivisions, ghost notes (busy 3–5: soft snares on the 16th either side of a backbeat, up to one, two or three a bar, never inside a fill or under double feel), how eagerly kicks chase the bass; hard (1–5) is loudness — one uniform velocity shift across the whole kit, hats never dropping back below audible; fills (off–5) alone decides how often fills fire and how big: 1–2 the quiet vocabulary (a negative fill, a flam, stabs — kick and snare together wherever the other tracks start notes in the last two beats, the band hit — and from 2 the 16th snare run), 3 adds the tom descent (two per drum down the kit, kick under the floor tom) and threes (3+3+2 over 16ths), 4–5 the full-bar riser (quarters → 8ths → 16ths) and the metal tier. Every fill is one idea in one direction on the 16th grid and lands on the next 1; the same fill never fires twice running. Phrase fills (fills 3–5): inside a section the Drummer also marks the phrase — a small fill (flam, negative, a snare pickup, a tom lick) on every 8th bar at fills 3, and on every 4th bar at 4–5 (8th bars may get a run, tom descent or threes); no crash follows a phrase fill — crashes are for section arrivals; follow picks what the kick listens to — any track, by name (its offbeat onsets and long notes, probabilistic; defaults to the detected bass) · chords (a kick exactly on every DECLARED chord change, deterministic, capped 4/bar) · off (skeleton and fills only); feel sets the backbeat rate — half (one big snare mid-bar, thinner hats: the slow-heavy read) · double (kick every beat, snare every offbeat 8th: the skank/D-beat read — bass-follow stands down, every slot is owned). The parts chips scope a reroll — kick, snare, hats, and fills (a role, not a pitch: the whole fill-and-crash layer at your section boundaries plus every phrase bar — the 4th, 8th, 12th… bar of a section — snare runs and tom descents included). Toggle any combination: all four = the normal full roll; "I love these snares, reroll just the kick" = tap snare and hats and fills off; fills alone rerolls just the fill bars — boundary and phrase bars (with fills set to off, that STRIPS the fills, phrase fills included, and lays plain groove — a de-fill). With the fills chip off the fills knob dims (nothing to control) and kept pieces stay byte-identical, hats capping under your real snares. Take chips now show their recipe (busy/hard/fills, ½ or 2× for feel, part initials when scoped) and still replay exactly. Layout: busy and hard are 1–5 segment rows (how much / how loud), fills off–5 and feel sit below, follow and parts under those — the long explanation you used to scroll past lives here in Help now. Two more rules: sections sharing an EXACT label share their groove — label two spans "A1" and the drums restate bar-for-bar (fills and crashes still vary: a fill is commentary on what comes next), so the same-label-same-color convention now also means same-drums; within a section the groove loops two bars and varies the fourth (bars 1 and 3 alike, bar 2 its answer, bar 4 the turnaround — kicks that follow the bass still follow each bar's actual bass); and at fills 4–5 the vocabulary opens its metal tier — a 16th-rate tom run down the kit, a full-bar crash-in kit fall, and a double-kick burst — weighted level with the run, the tom descent, threes and the riser, so the snare run still turns up at the top of the knob.
Edit menu
An Edit ▾ menu appears beside File on songs you can edit: Undo, Redo, Copy, Cut, Paste at cursor, Paste to… (another track, another octave), Delete, Split, ⇅ Transpose (chromatic, octave, and in-key moves live in its sheet), ♫ Insert chord, Move to track, 🥁 Drum pattern, Drummer, Divide, Join, Q Quantize — the same actions as the edit-row icons, labeled. The row itself keeps the heavy-use tools up front and folds the rest behind More (tap to expand; the choice sticks per device) — so pencil mode's duration picker fits on one iPad row again. Redo replays what Undo took back; making any new edit clears the Redo history (standard editor behavior).
Velocity
The vol slider in the edit row (1–127, the number beside it shows the value) sets the pencil's loudness — and with notes selected in Select mode, dragging it adjusts ALL of them live, brightness tracking the drag, committing one undo step when you let go. Tap a selection (or a single note) and the slider jumps to its current velocity — and when every selected note shares one velocity, the selection readout says so ("… 6 notes · vel 80").
Delete
Tap Delete in the edit row — every selected note goes at once. Delete key does the same.
Divide
Select notes, tap Divide, pick N (2–7): each note becomes N equal notes — pitch, velocity, and total span unchanged. This is how triplets happen: 3 on a half note = quarter-note triplets (the 1 · 1.667 · 2.333 placement no grid offers), 3 on a quarter = eighth triplets, 6 on a held two-beat note = a run of six. The pieces are free notes — drag them anywhere after.
Remove duplicate notes
Edit ▾ → Remove duplicate notes deletes notes stacked exactly on top of another on the same track (same start, same pitch) — you only ever hear one of them. The longer one stays; the status line says how many went, and one undo brings them back.
Save last 60s (Capture MIDI)
You were noodling on the keys with nothing recording and played something worth keeping. Tap Save last 60s at the end of the Instrument panel's bar (or Edit ▾ → 🎹 Keep that): the last minute of what you played on the on-screen keys or a MIDI keyboard lands on the selected track at the playhead, timed as you played it against the song's tempo from the cursor (a tempo change inside the phrase is honoured, and the speed slider applies the same way it does to Record), raw unless Settings → Other → Snap while recording is on. The whole phrase is one undo step, exactly like a take from Record. The app keeps only the last 60 seconds, forgets it the moment you open another song, and never saves it anywhere — nothing is written until you ask. On your own songs and note tracks only; the status line says why when it can't. No hardware key yet.
Velocity lane
View ▾ → Panels → Velocity lane opens a strip under the roll: one stalk per note at the note's start, its height the note's velocity (1–127), in the track's colour — the selected track bright, the others dim, lasso'd notes ringed gold. Drag a stalk up or down to set that note's velocity; the roll's note brightness follows your finger, and the number reads beside the stalk. Drag a stalk that is part of your selection and the whole selection scales together — the grabbed note lands where your finger is and every other note keeps its ratio to it, so a chord keeps its balance and an accent pattern survives a lift (clamped at 1 and 127). One undo step per drag, like the vol slider. Where two notes start on the same beat, a lasso'd note is the one you grab. On a chip capture the lane is read-only: those velocities — and the faint falling line a note's software envelope draws from its velocity down to where the chip lets it decay — are facts from the capture; use File ▾ → Edit a copy to shape them. Hidden in Tracks and Score views (it is the roll's lane); off by default, and whether it is open is remembered on this device. No hardware key.
Shape (volume inside a note)
A held note can get louder or softer while it sounds — one note, no new attack. Select notes, then ⋯ → Shape (or Edit ▾ → Shape…) and tap Swell (rises to the end), Fade (falls to a quarter), Swell–fade (rises to the middle, falls to half) or Flat (clears it). One tap shapes every selected note, scaled to each note's length; one undo undoes it. Velocity stays the attack, and the shape is stored relative to velocity — drag a stalk later and the whole shape moves with it (nothing goes above 127). A Swell or Swell–fade on a note that is already loud makes room: its velocity drops to 73 so the swell can still climb to 127, and the status line says how many notes it lowered (the same undo puts the velocity back). Fade and Flat never change velocity. In the Velocity lane a shaped note draws its line across its own width; with one note selected its points show gold handles — drag a point sideways to move it in time, up or down to change its level (one undo per drag). The shape moves and copies with its note (copy, paste, move to track) and is saved in the .mid as key pressure inside the note, so other apps keep it. You hear it on the synth voices (the chip waves and organ — with View ▾ → Mode → Hear the MIDI on a capture); sampled and game instruments, and the console voice, play their own envelope instead. A chip capture's notes carry the shape the chip itself played (FF1 Shop's pulse 2 swells inside each bar) as read-only facts. Ask can do it too: "shape the notes on pulse 2 in bars 25 to 28 with a swell". No hardware key.
Quantize (Q)
Select notes, tap Q Quantize (Edit ▾ → Quantize…, or the Q key) and pick a strength — 100% snaps each note's start exactly onto the current grid (the same grid Move/Pencil use: 16ths, triplet steps with a T duration active, or 32nds with 32nd picked or once the song has any); 75%/50% moves it only partway there, a soft nudge for a take that's close but not glued down. Also quantize note ends additionally snaps each note's END and resizes to match — off by default, so a raw take's durations stay as played and only the onsets straighten. This is Logic's Quantize command: it acts on a selection AFTER the fact, undoably (one undo step for the whole batch) — recording itself keeps what you played (see Record); Quantize is how you straighten it out when you want to.
Undo
The edit toolbar always starts with undo, redo, cut, copy, paste and delete, then ⋯ (transpose, chords, Drummer, Bassist, divide, join, split), and Select / Pencil / Erase with Pencil's note values sit on their own row below — so neither row moves when ⋯ opens. Every gesture above — move, resize, copy, paste, delete, divide — is exactly one undo step, and so is every track change: voice, color, volume, pan, M / S / H. Keyboard: ⌘Z undoes, ⇧⌘Z (or ⌘Y) redoes, when you're not typing in a box. Everything that adds or removes notes is one step — pencil, erase, paste, split, join, chord and progression inserts (bands included), a recorded take, Drummer and Bassist takes — and so is adding or deleting a track: ✕ Delete track is one undo away, and a kit or bass track a generator created leaves with its take. An annotation's Save or Delete in its window is one undo step too (every type: chord, section, note, key, tempo…). Every undo and redo says what it took back on the status line — undid: chord G7 · bar 13–14 (saved), undid: deleted section B · bar 17–24 — brings it into view if it's off screen, and flashes an annotation's bars in the ruler, so a text note never disappears without you seeing it.

Import…
File → Import… opens the import hub: one screen, one section per format Night Roll reads, each saying exactly what it needs with its own Choose files… (one shared picker underneath — a file lands in whichever section's byte-sniff it matches, whichever section you tapped from); a drop target in the hub takes dragged files too. MIDI (.mid, .midi, .smf, .kar, even RIFF-wrapped .rmi — multi-select capable): ask where they belong — Keep local (this-device draft, annotations never sync) or Create album — one or many MIDIs become an album folder under drafts, audition/rename/✕ like a chip capture, and ⇪ Commit publishes them to the songs repo so annotations sync everywhere (commit rewrites the .mid as notes+tempo+meter — foreign CCs/programs are not preserved). NES: the .nsf, plus its .m3u if the rip has one (track names) — an NSF that needs an expansion sound chip (VRC6, VRC7, FDS, MMC5, Namco 163, Sunsoft 5B) is refused by name ("expansion sound chip <names> not supported") — the NES pipeline captures only the 2A03, and those songs keep their lead voices on the extra chip. Game Boy: the .gbs, plus its .m3u, same rule. A rip with more than one chip file (a game shipped in two ROM revisions — Zophar's Game Boy Tetris is DMG-TRA-0.gbs and DMG-TRA-1.gbs) imports all of them in one go: each playlist line names its file, the first file by name is the album's, and a song from another file keeps that file beside it in the archive. Without a playlist, every file's slots are listed on their own. Super NES: every .spc of the album, picked together — tagged lengths, so the loop scan is skipped. Genesis: .vgm/.vgz — synth voices only, no console-audio render yet. PlayStation: every .minipsf plus the one .psflib, picked together — reads Sony's own SEQ/VAB driver and Square's AKAO (Final Fantasy VII/VIII/IX, Chrono Cross); both play the console's own audio. PlayStation 2: every .minipsf2/.psf2 plus the one .psf2lib, picked together — reads Sony's stock driver (SQ/HD/BD) and Square Enix's own (BGM/WD — Final Fantasy X); both play the console's own audio. A PS2 set that's only streamed audio (Ico, XIII) is refused: "this is streamed audio, not note data — Night Roll reads sequence data (notes), not pre-rendered streams". Nintendo 64: every .miniusf plus the one .usflib. For NES, Game Boy, PlayStation, PlayStation 2 and Nintendo 64: a playlist (.m3u) picked on its own, later, names an open song's album (or, mid-capture, renames an already-open session's tracks). ∿ Audio tracks (recordings)
A recording — a guitar take, a vocal, a phone memo, a DAW bounce — as a track next to your MIDI voices. Tap the ＋∿ chip in the track bar (compositions and local drafts; File → Import… also recognizes audio files) and pick wav, aiff, mp3, or m4a. The clip lands at the cursor's bar as a new track named after the file, with the usual chip: M, S, color, fader, rename, ✕ Delete track. Leading silence is trimmed on arrival, so the take's FIRST SOUND sits on that bar (the status line says how much it trimmed). It plays in time with the song, loops with it (a clip longer than the notes extends the song's end), and is in the exported audio. Slow it down and the pitch stays put: at any speed but 100% the take is re-rendered (time-stretched, the way a slow-downer app does it) in the background — the lane says "⏳ preparing 50% (pitch kept)…" for a moment, then it plays in tune with your notes; a device toggle in the recording sheet turns that off for tape-style, slower = lower, like chip audio. Fine for guitar and voice down to about 40%; grainier below. Tracks view shows the waveform; tap the clip to select it (the status line says file, length, where its bytes are, and where it starts), tap again to play from its start, and in Select mode hold and drag to slide it in time (16th snap, one undo undoes; with no tool armed a drag pans, as with notes). Every other view shows a thin ∿ strip under the ruler marking where the recording plays. Pieces: a recording can be cut into pieces that move on their own. Split at cursor (the edit row's Cut, Edit ▾, or the recording sheet) cuts the selected piece where the play cursor is; trim by dragging a selected piece's left or right edge in Select (left trim keeps the sound where it is); Erase tap on a piece removes just that piece (the track stays); each piece drags separately. One undo undoes any of it. Tap the selected chip for the recording sheet (‹ prev / next › step between pieces): nudge starts by a bar, a beat, or a 16th, offset by 10 ms or 100 ms (how far into the file the piece starts — the last hair of alignment), ⇤ Align first sound (re-trims leading silence to the current anchor), ♩ Tempo from this take (reads the beat spacing of the piece — its waveform's onsets, autocorrelated over 40–240 BPM — and offers the tempo it hears plus half and double, with how strong the read is; tap one and it becomes the song's tempo at bar 1, one undo undoes; your notes keep their bar positions, so the grid moves to meet the take, which is the point; needs about four seconds of steady playing, and your own songs only — captures keep their measured tempo), ♩♩ Map the bars to this take (the other direction: the GRID bends to the recording — it finds the beats through the whole piece, groups them into bars by the meter, and shows what it found: how many bars, the tempo range, and where the first downbeat sits; Apply writes one tempo change per bar from the piece's bar onward so every bar line lands on a downbeat it heard, wobble and all; ◀ downbeat / downbeat ▶ shift which beat counts as bar 1 when it guessed wrong — the correction Logic's Smart Tempo asks for too; the piece must start on a bar line, and a first downbeat that isn't at the piece's start means a pickup: shift, or trim the piece to the downbeat first; one undo removes the whole map), Split at cursor, Remove piece, Replace file…, and ☐ someone else's recording: tick it for a band's track you play along with and it is never uploaded — GitHub removes such files on a rights holder's notice, and a public site is distribution. Storage: until you Save, the bytes live on this device only (the sheet says so; Safari can evict browser storage after a week away, so Save soon); Save writes them beside the song as <song>.audio/<file> — into your folder in folder mode, into the songs repo otherwise. Files over 20 MB get one question: store as 16-bit mono WAV (lossless for a mono source, half a stereo bounce) or import as-is. m4a/mp3 note: Safari and Chrome trim the encoder's lead-in differently, so millisecond alignment is honest only for WAV/AIFF. Not yet: recording from the mic, trimming or splitting a clip, looping a clip by itself, keeping pitch when slowing down, iPad/Safari playback of webm.

AI
Talk to an AI tutor about what you're looking at. Tap AI in the top bar. Every message you send carries the song, your cursor, the lasso, your own annotations, and the notes of the bars in view (or the ruler-selected range), so "what's happening in bar 9?" means YOUR bar 9 — bars and beats are the ones on your ruler, in your declared meter. Say the word and it will also add, edit, delete, or publish an annotation — never on its own; ask it to change or take back the wrong one and it edits or deletes that exact annotation in place (never a duplicate left beside it), and asking it to publish runs the same Publish the footer button does. Ask can also write notes on your own songs when you ask it to — spell it out ("a C3 gallop on the triangle in bar 5" means an eighth note plus two sixteenths, not the word "gallop") and it lands at once as one undo step; it never volunteers a note you didn't dictate. Ask can repeat bars: "repeat bars 5–6 after bar 6" copies them and moves the rest later, on every track, as one undo step. Ask can also run the Drummer on your own song — the same generator as the Drummer button, never hand-written hits: say the bars or one of your section labels and what you want ("redo the intro following pulse1 and pulse2 instead of the triangle", "the A part again with a bit less energy", "bars 9–16, fills off, half feel"). It replaces only that range's drum hits, as one undo step; the reply names the seed, so "that one again, quieter" replays the same take with one knob changed, and the take shows up in the Drummer sheet's take chips. Energy sets busy and hard together; busy, hard, fills, feel, parts and follow (which tracks the kick listens to — any tracks by name, chords, or off) can each be named on their own. And it can act in the app: "go to bar 13 and play", "stop", "loop bars 5 to 12", "undo that" — several in one breath run in order, and a quick one answers with a single line and no further reply. The full list, with a phrase for each, is AI commands below.
✦ Annotate this song…
Normal mode only — in Learning mode the item does not exist: the analysis is yours to make. Tap View ▾ → Mode → ✦ Annotate this song… (no keyboard shortcut) to ask the AI model from Settings for estimates of this song's sections, chords and key, read from the notes plus computed facts (which bars repeat, how the bass moves, ranges, rhythm). Tick Sections, Chords or Key to choose what it may write, then Run — nothing is written before that tap, and never on its own. A run is one undo; Clear AI annotations removes every tagged one at once (also one undo). A long song goes to the model in bar windows and the answers merge; a reply the app cannot read writes nothing. They are estimates: check them by ear before you keep them. Unsynced until you Publish, like any annotation. Switch back to Learning mode and every ✦ AI annotation is hidden, never deleted — gone from the roll, All notes, the LCD key and the ✦ Ask context until you switch to Normal again.
House rules
🎓 Learning mode (Settings → Other): hints and direction first — it confirms or refines a guess you've made, and tells you outright only when you insist or say you give up; general theory questions it answers directly; it never reveals a key you haven't set. THE ONE-SCREEN LIST

Not a form — a checklist
A full analysis covers sixteen things in five groups, in the order a working analyst takes them: form first, because every other item is asked phrase by phrase, and form is what tells you where the phrases are. Read the list once, mentally tick it off against your own annotations, then open any section below for the full explanation of one line. Nothing here names a key, a chord, or a reading of any real song — it's reference text, the same for every song you open.
Form
1 Sections — where the music starts over or changes idea. 2 Phrase lengths — how many bars each one takes. 3 Phrase pairing — do phrases come in question-and-answer pairs, or repeat with a changed ending?
Harmony
4 Key and chords. 5 Roman numerals — what job each chord does in the key. 6 Cadences — where a phrase comes to rest, and how. 7 The bass line, read as its own melody. 8 Where the harmony pulls (tension) and where it settles (release).
Melody
9 Which scale degree the tune lands on at phrase ends. 10 The melody's shape — steps or leaps, rising or falling, where the high point sits. 11 Motifs — short ideas that return changed.
Texture & rhythm
12 Which voice carries the tune, which the harmony, which the bass — and where they swap. 13 The rhythm and the groove — any ostinato, how often the chords change, where the pattern breaks. 14 What the music is doing in the scene it plays under.
Summary
15 What the piece does and why it works, in your own words. 16 How it compares with the same composer's other pieces.

FORM

Sections, phrases, and pairing
What to find: A phrase is the smallest complete musical sentence — long enough to make a point, short enough to breathe in one go; most game loops build theirs in 4 or 8 bars, so a 3- or 5-bar phrase is worth a note the moment you spot it. A section is one or more phrases that share an idea (the same material, or the same job — intro, loop body, turnaround); it ends where the idea changes or the music starts over. Phrases often come in pairs: an antecedent (the "question" phrase, ending open) answered by a consequent (the "answer" phrase, ending closed) — together the two make a period. A sentence is the other common pairing: a short idea stated, immediately repeated, then pushed on into a new, often faster-moving continuation. A period often starts both phrases the same way and changes only the ending — open the first time, closed the second (a parallel period).

How to find it in Night Roll: Drag along the bar ruler to hear a candidate phrase on its own — a drag arms the cycle and loops just that span, so you can audition "does this sound like one idea?" without re-selecting. Once you're sure, select the range and + Note → section, with a short label (A, B, A′…); sections nest by containment, so an 8-bar idea built from two 4-bar phrases can be one section with two inside it, and the ruler's ▸ chevron folds between those levels.

How to write it down: The section band's label carries the form letter; its attached note (the ✱ on the band) is where "ends open" or "answers the first phrase" goes — add the bar count too if it isn't a round 4 or 8.

Common mistakes: Counting a pickup (a note or two before the first full bar) as bar 1, which throws off every phrase count after it. Calling two phrases a question-and-answer pair just because they're the same length — pairing is about where each one RESTS, not its size. Stopping at "there's an A and a B" without writing how many bars each one takes.

HARMONY

Key, numerals, cadences, bass, tension
What to find: A chord's name (Gm, Cmaj7) is the surface; a Roman numeral (i, IV, V7) says what JOB that chord does in its key — I/i is home, V pulls toward home, IV leans away from it — and the same job sounds the same in any key, which is why numerals, not letter names, are what let you compare two songs. A cadence is where a phrase comes to rest, and the KIND of rest is the finding: an authentic cadence (V to I/i) closes all the way; a half cadence (ending on V) stops short, open, mid-sentence; a plagal cadence (IV to I) is a softer close, the "amen" motion; a deceptive cadence sets up V and then lands somewhere else (often vi), denying the close you expected. Underneath the chords, the bass line is worth reading as its own melody — a bass that walks down by step, or leaps to outline the chord, is a decision, not an accident. Tension and release is the harmony's push and pull across a whole phrase: which chord pulls hardest (usually V, or a chord borrowed from outside the key), and where it's allowed to let go.

How to find it in Night Roll: Lasso the notes sounding at a moment and tap Chord? to see what they spell; Challenge? on an existing chord band shows the evidence — which label tones sound, which are missing, what else is there — so you can check a guess instead of trusting it blind. The ◯5 Circle of fifths (View ▾ → Tools) lays out every key's chords around the wheel and shows which ones are close neighbors, useful once you know the key and want to see a chord's relatives. The key itself lives on the transport display — tap it to open the key: annotation (or "mode?" if you have the tonic but not the mode yet).

How to write it down: A chord band's own label is the chord name; its attached note is where the Roman numeral and any "borrowed" comment goes. A cadence is a text note at the exact beat it lands — "half, stops on V" or "authentic" — or a note on the section band if the cadence is what defines the section's end. The bass-as-melody and tension/release findings are each a text note spanning the phrase they describe.

Common mistakes: Naming the chord and stopping — a letter name with no numeral tells you the color, not the job. Calling every chord change a cadence; a cadence is specifically where a PHRASE rests, not every harmonic move along the way. Writing "tense" or "resolved" without saying which chord or beat did it. Numbering the numeral off the piece's key signature instead of the CURRENT key — a key change mid-song moves what counts as "home," so last section's IV can be this section's I.

MELODY

Scale degrees, shape, motifs
What to find: At each phrase end (and often each downbeat), note which scale degree — which numbered step of the key's scale, 1 through 7 — the tune is landing on: 1 closes a phrase fully, 5 and 2 leave it open, 7 leans hard toward 1 without quite arriving. Separately, describe the melody's shape: is it mostly steps (one scale degree to its neighbor) or leaps, mostly rising or falling, and where does the highest note of the phrase sit — once, or repeated? A motif is a short recurring idea — a few notes, a rhythm, a shape — that comes back CHANGED: moved up a step, turned upside down, stretched into longer notes. Finding one means naming it and then listing every place it returns, and how each return differs from the first statement.

How to find it in Night Roll: find: (View ▾ → Tools) lights up every occurrence of one pitch class across every visible track at once — built for exactly this: pick the note you think a motif turns on and watch where it recurs, with everything else dimmed so a confident zero is still an answer. Reading the notes strip while the song plays catches a shape by ear before you go hunting for it by eye.

How to write it down: The scale-degree and shape findings are a text note spanning the phrase. A motif gets one note per return — anchor each at the bar where it starts, with what changed this time ("same shape, augmented" or "inverted, now in the bass").

Common mistakes: Calling every repeated idea a motif — a motif has to come back CHANGED to be worth tracking; an identical repeat is just a repeat. Describing shape in isolation from the scale degrees ("it goes up" tells you less than "it climbs from 3 to 5 to the leading tone"). Finding a motif's first appearance and never going back for the rest of its returns. Mistaking a passing decoration — a quick step off a held note and straight back — for a motif; a motif is an idea worth naming, not every ornament.

TEXTURE & RHYTHM

Roles, groove, the scene
What to find: At any moment, which voice carries the TUNE (the melody role), which fills in the HARMONY (chords, arpeggios, repeated notes — the accompaniment role), and which holds the BASS (the lowest, usually slowest-moving voice) — and where those roles swap between tracks, which is itself worth a note. For rhythm: is there a groove or ostinato (a short pattern repeated steadily underneath); how often do the chords actually change (every bar, every beat, every two bars); and is there a bar where that pattern visibly breaks — a fill, a held note, a rest where you expected an attack? Last, ask what the SCENE under the music needs — tension, calm, forward motion, danger — and how the music supplies it; this one has no music-theory name, it's just asking what the piece is FOR.

How to find it in Night Roll: Mute tracks one at a time (the M on each track) to isolate a voice and hear its role alone; the Tracks view lays every track out as a lane so you can see at a glance which one is busiest where. Nothing in the app names a role for you — this one is entirely by ear and by eye.

How to write it down: These findings are mostly song-level rather than tied to one bar, so a text note at bar 1 (or spanning the section where roles swap) is the usual home; a role swap at a specific bar gets its own note right there instead.

Common mistakes: Assuming the top voice is always the melody — accompaniment figures often sit high too. Noting the groove once and never checking whether it holds for the whole piece. Describing the scene for a game you haven't actually watched the music against — if you don't know the scene, say so instead of guessing. Confusing a role swap with a texture change — the same three roles can still be in play even when which track plays which one has swapped.

SUMMARY

What it does, and the comparison
What to find: One plain paragraph, in your own words: what the piece does and why it works — not a list of the fifteen things above restated, but the one or two ideas that tie them together (a form that keeps undercutting its own cadences, say, or a bass line doing more work than the melody). Then put it beside another piece by the same composer: a shared habit — the same cadence type favored, the same kind of bass motion, the same loop plan — is usually the more durable lesson than anything true of just one piece alone.

How to find it in Night Roll: The album list puts every piece by one composer a tap apart, so the comparison costs nothing but attention. There's no tool for the summary itself — it's the one place where you're not reading an annotation back, you're writing the conclusion the other fifteen things were building toward.

How to write it down: A text note at bar 1, or wherever your notes-strip habit keeps song-level thoughts.

Common mistakes: Summarizing the form again instead of saying why it works. Comparing two pieces on a surface detail (both happen to be in a minor key) instead of a compositional habit. Writing the summary before doing the other four groups — it reads thin, and it usually has to be rewritten once the harmony and melody findings are actually in.

GETTING BETTER

A ladder, not a syllabus
Four rungs, each small enough to actually finish. One group at a time: analyze a whole piece with ONLY one group from the list above — say, every cadence and nothing else. Then the next piece, two groups. Doing all sixteen at once on piece one is how analysis stops happening.
Go back a month later: re-read an old set of notes and add the one thing you skipped the first time — most often the Roman numeral on every chord, since that's the item that takes longest to get fluent at.
Compare two pieces, side by side: same composer, different pieces, same list — the album view makes this one tap each way.
Ask the tutor, not for the answer: five questions that stay inside Learning mode's rule (hints first, your own finding stands) because they ask about the CONCEPT, not your song: "what makes a cadence feel closed instead of open?" · "what's the difference between a motif and just a repeated note?" · "when does a leap in a melody feel like a question?" · "what's the difference between a period and a sentence?" · "how do I tell tension from just 'a chord I don't recognize'?" A tutor answer is a hint toward your OWN listening, not a verdict to copy into a note — the annotation still has to be the thing you heard.

A WORKED EXAMPLE

A made-up eight-bar loop
Invented for this page — not a real song, not anyone's composition. Picture a made-up 8-bar loop in C major (any key would do), phrased as two 4-bar halves.
Form: A (bars 1–4), A′ (bars 5–8) — the same phrase twice, the second time with a changed ending. Open first, closed second: that makes it a parallel period (question, then answer).
Numerals and cadences: bars 1–4 run I – vi – IV – V, stopping on V: a half cadence, open, so the ear expects more. Bars 5–8 start the same, I – vi, then IV – V – I with the V and I sharing the last two bars: an authentic cadence, closed. The "changed ending" IS the cadence change — half the first time, authentic the second.
The bass line: C, A, F, G under the first half; C, A, F, G, C under the second — playing the chord roots, so it reads as a simple tune of its own, not just "whatever's under the chord."
Summary: A loop that answers itself — the first four bars ask a question by stopping on V, and the last four repeat the question but this time step from V to I and land home. The whole piece's trick is that one changed chord in the last bar does the job a whole new phrase would otherwise have to do.
